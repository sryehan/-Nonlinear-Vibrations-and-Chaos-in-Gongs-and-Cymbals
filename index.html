Study objectives

Why do gongs sing so wildly?

Gongs and cymbals are among the most physically complex percussion instruments. Their vibratory displacement becomes comparable to the plate thickness — triggering geometric nonlinearity that makes linear acoustics fundamentally inadequate.

This bibliographic study, following Chaigne, Touzé & Thomas (2005), aims to summarise the experimental measurements and theoretical models that explain these rich dynamics — providing a rigorous foundation for realistic sound synthesis.

  • Summarise experiments: frequency responses, modal coupling, chaos transitions at large amplitudes.
  • Present governing nonlinear equations (von Kármán plate theory with quadratic & cubic coupling terms).
  • Reproduce main dynamical features through MATLAB simulation of coupled-mode oscillators.

⚡
Geometric nonlinearity: When plate mid-surface deflection $w$ becomes comparable to thickness $h$, in-plane membrane stresses couple to bending — making the restoring force nonlinear. The von Kármán plate equations capture this: $\varepsilon = \partial_x u + \frac{1}{2}(\partial_x w)^2$. This quadratic strain term is the root of all the rich dynamics: amplitude-dependent frequency shifts, internal resonance, and eventually chaos.

Theory I — governing equations

Von Kármán Plate Equations

Transverse equation of motion
$$\rho h \ddot{w} + D\Delta^2 w = L(w,F) + p(x,t)$$
$D = Eh^3/12(1-\nu^2)$ is flexural rigidity; $L(w,F)$ is the von Kármán bracket coupling bending $w$ and Airy stress $F$.
Compatibility equation (Airy stress)
$$\frac{1}{Eh}\Delta^2 F = -\frac{1}{2}L(w,w)$$
The right-hand side is quadratic in $w$ — this is the geometric nonlinearity that couples modes and generates harmonic content.
Von Kármán bracket
$$L(f,g) = f_{xx}g_{yy}+f_{yy}g_{xx}-2f_{xy}g_{xy}$$
The bracket vanishes for purely linear bending — it activates only when in-plane membrane stresses are significant, i.e., when $w \sim h$.
Theory II — modal reduction

Coupled Oscillator Model

Reduced-order ODEs (Eq. 9, Chaigne et al.)
$$\ddot{q}_p + 2\mu_p\dot{q}_p + \omega_p^2 q_p = \varepsilon\!\sum_{rs}\!\Gamma_{prs}^{(1)}q_rq_s + \varepsilon\!\sum_{rst}\!\Gamma_{prst}^{(2)}q_rq_sq_t$$
$\Gamma^{(1)}$ (quadratic) and $\Gamma^{(2)}$ (cubic) coupling coefficients are projections of the von Kármán bracket onto linear eigenmodes. Computed from MATLAB modal analysis.
Simulation system (3-mode, driven)
$$\ddot{x}_3 + 2\mu_3\dot{x}_3 + \omega_3^2 x_3 = \varepsilon(-\beta_{11}x_1^2 - \beta_{22}x_2^2 + P\cos\Omega t)$$
Mode 3 ($\omega_3 \approx 8.1$) is externally driven at frequency $\Omega = 8.1$. Modes 1 & 2 ($\omega_{1,2}\approx 4.0$) are enslaved via 2:1 internal resonance — a classic route to chaos.
Internal resonance condition
$$\omega_3 \approx \omega_1 + \omega_2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{combination resonance}$$
When this relationship is satisfied, energy injected into mode 3 is efficiently transferred to modes 1 and 2. This mechanism is responsible for the "spreading" spectrogram of a struck gong.

Gong Free-Vibration Response — Time Domain

FIG. 1 — Free-vibration analytical simulation · Blue: total displacement envelope · Orange: nonlinearly-coupled mode response · Time axis in seconds · Amplitude normalised
What we see
Delayed onset
The orange coupled-mode response (modes 1 & 2, ω ≈ 4 rad/s) does not activate immediately. It grows gradually as energy fed into the driven mode 3 builds up above the internal-resonance threshold — a hallmark of nonlinear energy transfer.
Saturation regime (~40–45 s)
Maximum coupling
Around t = 40–45 s, both responses reach comparable amplitudes (≈ 0.22). This is the chaotic saturation region — the regime where a struck gong produces its signature crash and shimmer.
Physical interpretation: The blue envelope represents all modes together; the orange shows only the slave modes. The large blue-only zone (before ~30 s) is pure linear ringing. The broadening and amplitude competition afterward is the signature of geometric nonlinearity activating combination resonance — exactly what Chaigne et al. describe as the transition to chaos.

Acoustic Excitation
Band-limited pseudo-random noise (30–300 Hz, two repetitions) excited the gong. The playRec function captured both electrical drive and acoustic radiation simultaneously. Welch averaging over N_fft = 2¹⁷ yielded the power spectrum overlaid with vibrometry data in the FRF1.png figure.
🔬
Laser Doppler Vibrometry
A scanning LDV measured surface velocity at a grid of points on the gong face. The resulting scan_random.mat file contains (x, y, z_velocity) triples for each frequency of interest. Delaunay triangulation and natural-neighbour griddata interpolation reconstructed smooth mode-shape surfaces.
🧮
Nonlinear ODE Integration
MATLAB ode45 integrated the 6-DOF coupled-oscillator system over 7.5 s with initial conditions x₁=x₃=0.1. Results were interpolated to 400 uniform frames for the animation. The internal resonance condition ω₃ ≈ ω₁+ω₂ was deliberately satisfied to trigger energy transfer into slave modes.

Conclusion

Chaos is not disorder —
it is rich determinism

From a single struck gong plate, geometric nonlinearity generates modal coupling, internal resonance, quasi-periodicity, and ultimately chaotic energy redistribution. The three-mode ODE model captures this route faithfully — and the lab data confirms it. The physics of a gong crash is, at its core, the story of the von Kármán bracket activating when displacement meets thickness.
